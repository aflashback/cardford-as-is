Review snapshot · 30 September 2026

How the game thinks.
What still needs proof.

CardFord can run the games and give short help during play. Its rules are dependable locally. Its more ambitious card planning is built for testing, but we still need stronger evidence that it makes the bots and coach better.

This page translates the current code, game samples and plans into decisions you can review. It describes the local project, not a public launch.

01 / Current state

What exists today

The game engine supports Spades by yourself, Spades with a teammate, and Hearts. Each game has its own rules. A real person plays against three trained computer players in the current table.

Rules and scores

Working

The server deals cards, checks allowed moves, decides each four-card round, and calculates bids, points and penalties. Solo and teammate games score differently.

Bots and coach

Working locally

Both use a shared decision analysis from the acting player's view. The coach can save its original advice even when live hints are turned off.

Deeper planning

Trial

Named trial versions can compare several legal choices and think ahead through possible hidden deals. The normal game still uses the established code policy.

Scenario library

Partial

Spades has named situations, example cases, partner clues and short coach messages. Many suggested partner decisions still lack independent review and real outcome checks.

Jev model

Not active

The server connection and offline comparison setup exist. One small live request returned an answer the validator rejected. Jev did not play in the recent card experiments.

Four real people

Future

The design has a separate table type for this. Matchmaking, access rules and the full four-person experience have not been built into the current local table.

02 / Decision path

What the bot is allowed to know

The coach thinks from the same limited view a player has. It does not inspect the other three hands to make a move.

Exact rule or visible fact Judgment that still needs testing Private information boundary

What is certain?

If someone cannot play a heart when hearts were led, we know they have no hearts left. If a king wins a round, we do not automatically know where the ace is.

What is a guess?

A high card saved for later, a teammate’s choice of suit, or an opponent’s likely next play can suggest a plan. Those clues need alternatives and exceptions, not a claim of certainty.

Same cards, different goal

Solo or with a teammate?

A “trick” means one round in which each of four players puts down one card. A “bid” is how many such rounds you promise to win. “Nil” means promising to win none.

Your score belongs to you

All three other seats compete against you. The bot checks your promised number of rounds, extra-round penalties, and whether taking or giving away a round helps you most.

05 / Keeping decisions reviewable

A shared set of situation cards

The reusable structure can serve later card games, while each game keeps its own rules, scoring and examples.

Each situation should connect what the player can see to an exact fact or cautious guess, the choice the bot makes, the short message the coach may show, and the evidence that supports both.

1 · SituationWhat happened, in this game and mode?
2 · Visible cluesYour cards, past rounds and known bids
3 · ChoicesPossible moves, reasons and exceptions
4 · Coach cueShort, truthful wording or silence
5 · EvidenceReplay case, counterexample and review
Already present

Spades situation names, public-card facts, saved analysis, example cases, cue text and selectable trial planners.

Still missing

A complete reviewed chain from each suggested partner clue to a tested decision, exception and accurate cue; independent human labels and fresh performance results.

06 / Suggested sequence

What to improve next

Move from concrete examples to reviewed choices, then measure outcomes. Keep Solo and teammate results separate.

Resume the saved sample when approved

Three of eight new hands are complete. Resume the three saved hands, then play the two high-card-gap setups. Exact stopping points and replay commands are saved. No collection is running.

First

Review the important decisions

For each situation, list the reasonable moves, what could go wrong, and whether a coach message would help. Have an independent game reviewer check them without seeing the bot’s answer.

Next

Improve one family at a time

Start with promises before play, then getting the lead back, protecting a teammate’s zero-round promise, and late-game score choices. Keep each change named and reversible.

Then

Run fair head-to-head games

Use the same fresh starting deals against several opponent styles. Report points, fulfilled promises, zero-round outcomes, extra-round penalties and mistaken coach claims for each mode.

Proof gate

Test Jev only after its connection is understood

Reproduce the rejected answer safely, then make a small, cost-limited comparison. Give Jev only the same visible information and legal choices. Compare its play separately from its explanation.

Later

Check whether people actually learn

Ask experienced players to make fresh choices later without help. Better outcomes with live cues alone do not show that the coach taught a lasting skill.
